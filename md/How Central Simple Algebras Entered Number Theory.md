## The question

The rings of integers of number fields are commutative. How could their study lead to noncommutative structures?

**Short answer.** The noncommutative algebras did not come from the rings of integers. They came from **norms**. A central simple algebra is a way of packaging the question "is a a norm from L?" as a single algebraic object. Once people saw this, around 1930–32, algebras became a working tool of class field theory. Their content survives today as the cohomology group H².

---

## 1. The basic idea: cyclic algebras encode norms

Let L/K be a cyclic extension of degree n with Galois group generated by σ, and let a ∈ K*. Take the K-algebra generated by L and one new element u, with relations

- uⁿ = a
- u·x = σ(x)·u for all x ∈ L.

This is Dickson's **cyclic algebra** (L/K, σ, a), a central simple K-algebra of dimension n² that contains L as a maximal commutative subfield. [R] Dickson himself called these "algebras of type D". Albert dates the construction to 1905, and Dickson's paper appeared in 1906 [R, W]. The notation (L|K, σ, a) seems to be Hasse's [R].

All the ingredients are commutative: a field L, a Galois automorphism σ and a scalar a. The only noncommutativity is the rule u·x = σ(x)·u, which records the Galois action.

**Key fact:** (L/K, σ, a) ≅ Mₙ(K), i.e. the algebra "splits", **if and only if a is a norm from L** [R].

For n = 2 you can see it directly. The reduced norm of x + yu is N(x) − a·N(y). If a = N(c), then c + u has reduced norm 0, so it is a zero divisor. For cyclic L/K this gives

  Br(L/K) ≅ K*/N(L*).

The right side is the **norm residue group**, which is exactly the object of class field theory. So any statement about which elements are norms is also a statement about which algebras split, and the reverse holds too.

### Why a noncommutative object at all?

*(Expanded 17 September 2026.)*

Take a Galois extension L/K with group G. Two commutative objects are in play:

1. **The field L**, a commutative ring.
2. **The group G**, abelian in the cyclic case that class field theory needs, and in any case a group acting on L by ring automorphisms.

Neither is noncommutative. But class field theory is not about L alone or G alone. It is about their **interaction**: which elements of K are norms, how primes decompose, what the Frobenius elements do. To study the interaction one wants a single ring that contains L and in which G acts by *inner* operations, so that "apply σ to x" is an algebraic operation inside the ring rather than an external symmetry.

**The minimal construction.** Adjoin to L one invertible element u_σ for each σ ∈ G, with the rule

  u_σ · x = σ(x) · u_σ  for all x ∈ L.

This single relation is the whole source of the noncommutativity, and it is forced: if u_σ commuted with x, conjugation by u_σ would be the identity, not σ. So the ring can be commutative only if G acts trivially. **Noncommutativity is exactly the price of internalising the Galois action**, no more and no less. The L-span of the u_σ has dimension n = |G| over L, hence n² over K, and L sits inside as a maximal commutative subalgebra with G acting on it by conjugation.

**The factor system.** What remains is how the u_σ multiply among themselves. One would like u_σ u_τ = u_{στ}, but associativity only forces

  u_σ u_τ = a_{σ,τ} · u_{στ}  with a_{σ,τ} ∈ L*,

and the a_{σ,τ} must satisfy a compatibility condition (Noether's equations). In the cyclic case G = ⟨σ⟩ this collapses to one scalar: u^n = a ∈ K*. That is Dickson's cyclic algebra (L/K, σ, a) above, and Noether's crossed product in general.

**The payoff.** The algebra (L/K, σ, a) is a matrix algebra Mₙ(K) exactly when a is a norm from L (the Key fact above). So the arithmetic question "is a ∈ N(L*)?", which is the central question of class field theory since K*/N(L*) is the norm residue group, has become the structural question "is this algebra split?". The structural question can be attacked with Wedderburn theory, localised ℘-adically (Hasse 1931, §2(d)) and glued back together (Brauer–Hasse–Noether 1932, §2(e)). Two commutative ingredients, one twisting rule, and the noncommutative object that results is a **device for measuring the failure of a to be a norm**.

**The modern residue.** The factor system a_{σ,τ} is a 2-cocycle, and the crossed product is what one gets by building a ring out of a 2-cocycle of G with values in L*. This is the isomorphism Br(L/K) ≅ H²(G, L*) [R]. It also explains why the algebras later receded from the foundations of class field theory (§3): the cohomology group carries the same data without needing to be a ring. But the reason they arose is that a ring holding both L and the action of G is the most concrete form that data can take.

### The universal object and what it acts on

*(Added 17 September 2026.)*

The construction above is in two steps: first adjoin the symbols u_σ with u_σ x = σ(x) u_σ, then decide how the u_σ multiply among themselves. So there is a universal object in the background, at two levels.

**1. No relations among the u_σ.** In the cyclic case, adjoining a single u with u·x = σ(x)·u and nothing else gives the **skew polynomial ring** L[u; σ] (an Ore extension): a left L-module with basis 1, u, u², …, with multiplication determined by u x = σ(x) u. The cyclic algebra is the quotient

  (L/K, σ, a) = L[u; σ] / (uⁿ − a).

The element uⁿ − a is *central* in L[u; σ], since uⁿ commutes with L (σⁿ = 1) and a ∈ K, so the ideal it generates is two-sided. For general G one gets the analogous skew ring over the free monoid on G.

**2. The obvious relations.** Imposing u_σ u_τ = u_{στ}, the trivial factor system, gives the **skew group ring** L#G (also written L * G): the free left L-module on the u_σ, with u_σ x = σ(x) u_σ. This is the crossed product with a_{σ,τ} = 1, i.e. (L/K, σ, 1) in the cyclic case.

These rings contain L and are free as L-modules, but they are not L-*algebras*: L is not central in them, which is the whole point. They are K-algebras.

**The natural action is on L itself.** Both rings act on L as a K-vector space: x ∈ L acts by multiplication and u_σ acts by σ. The two rules are compatible precisely because u_σ·x = σ(x)·u_σ is the operator identity σ ∘ (mult by x) = (mult by σ(x)) ∘ σ. This gives a K-algebra map

  L#G → End_K(L),

and it is an **isomorphism**. Injectivity is Dedekind's independence of characters: if Σ x_σ σ = 0 as a map L → L then all x_σ = 0. Both sides have K-dimension n². So

  L#G ≅ End_K(L) ≅ Mₙ(K).

The universal crossed product with trivial cocycle is literally the ring of all K-linear endomorphisms of L, and u_σ is the Galois automorphism σ viewed as a K-linear operator. This is consistent with the Key fact: a = 1 is a norm, so (L/K, σ, 1) splits. The skew polynomial ring L[u; σ] also acts on L (u ↦ σ), now with kernel (uⁿ − 1). More generally L[u; σ] acts on any L-vector space with a σ-semilinear endomorphism; that is exactly what an L[u; σ]-module is.

**Modules over the crossed product.** This gives a conceptual reading of the whole story.

- A module over L#G is an L-vector space V with a semilinear G-action, σ(xv) = σ(x)σ(v). That is **Galois descent datum**. Galois descent says every such V is L ⊗_K V₀ with V₀ = V^G. In ring language: Mod(L#G) ≃ Mod(K), which is the Morita equivalence L#G ≅ Mₙ(K) again, with L as the standard module.
- A module over (L/K, G, a) is an L-vector space V with σ-semilinear maps u_σ satisfying u_σ u_τ = a_{σ,τ} u_{στ}: **twisted descent datum**. The cocycle measures the obstruction to descending.

**The Key fact from this viewpoint.** Set A = (L/K, σ, a), central simple of dimension n². Such an algebra is Mₙ(K) iff it has a module of K-dimension n, i.e. of L-dimension 1. (By Wedderburn, A ≅ M_r(D) with D a division algebra of dimension d² and rd = n. The unique simple A-module is Dʳ, of K-dimension rd² = nd, and every module is a sum of copies of it. So the smallest nonzero module has dimension nd, which equals n iff d = 1 iff A ≅ Mₙ(K). Compare the note *Why n divides dim_k I for a right ideal*.) A one-dimensional L-space with a σ-semilinear u is V = L with u(x) = c·σ(x) for some c ∈ L*. Then

  uⁿ(x) = c·σ(c)·σ²(c)⋯σⁿ⁻¹(c)·x = N_{L/K}(c)·x,

so the relation uⁿ = a can be satisfied exactly when a = N(c) for some c. The zero-divisor argument above (c + u has reduced norm 0 when N(c) = a) is the same fact seen through the reduced norm.

**Further afield.** The same skew polynomial ring L[u; σ] appears wherever a field comes with a distinguished automorphism: with σ = Frobenius on the Witt vectors W(k) it is the **Dieudonné ring**, and its modules (Dieudonné modules, φ-modules, difference modules) are again vector spaces with a semilinear operator. The twisting rule u x = σ(x) u is the one piece of structure all of these share.

## 2. The history in several strands

### (a) Quaternions and quadratic forms (19th century)

- **Hamilton** (1843) found the quaternions *(unchecked)*. **Frobenius** (Crelle 84, 1878) proved that ℝ, ℂ and ℍ are the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over ℝ [W].
- **Lipschitz** (1886) began the arithmetic of integral quaternions, in connection with sums of squares [W]. **Hurwitz** ("Über die Zahlentheorie der Quaternionen", Göttinger Nachr. 1896 [AH]; book 1919 [W]) enlarged the order to what are now called the Hurwitz integers and used it for the four-square theorem.
- **Legendre** (1785) gave a necessary and sufficient condition for ax² + by² + cz² = 0 to have nontrivial integer solutions [W]. *Modern reading:* the quaternion algebra (a, b) over ℚ splits exactly when z² = ax² + by² has a nontrivial solution, so Legendre's criterion is a local-global principle for quaternion algebras. This is an interpretation, not a historical claim.
- **Hilbert's norm residue symbol** (*Zahlbericht*, 1897): (a, b)_v = +1 exactly when b is a local norm from K_v(√a), i.e. when (a, b) splits at v. For quadratic extensions, the local-global principle for norms goes back to the *Zahlbericht* [R]. The product formula ∏_v (a, b)_v = 1 contains quadratic reciprocity *(standard; not checked against Hilbert's text)*. Hasse later proved the product formula for the general m-th Hilbert symbol (Crelle 158, 1927), and it contains the m-th power reciprocity laws [R].

In algebra language, the quadratic product formula says a quaternion algebra over ℚ ramifies at an even number of places. That is the first case of "the invariants sum to zero".

### (b) Structure theory of algebras (1900s–1920s)

- **Wedderburn**: finite division rings are commutative (Trans. AMS 6, 1905); every semisimple algebra is a direct sum of simple algebras, each a matrix algebra over a division algebra ("On hypercomplex numbers", Proc. LMS (2) 6, 1907) [W].
- **Dickson**: cyclic algebras (Trans. AMS 7, 1906) [W, R].
- **Brauer** introduced similarity classes of central simple algebras. They form a group under tensor product, every element has finite order (the "exponent"), and the exponent divides the index. His papers appeared in Math. Z. 28 (1928) and Math. Z. 30 (1929), with a report at the 1928 DMV meeting [R]. Some of this was already in Schur (1919) [R, citing Lorenz]. **The name "Brauer group" was coined by Hasse in the BHN paper (1932)** [R]. Brauer had also defined factor systems and used them systematically. Such systems had appeared earlier in Schur and Dickson [R].
- **Noether** simplified Brauer's factor systems to her **crossed products** (*verschränkte Produkte*) for Galois splitting fields, with the "Noether equations" as associativity conditions. She presented them in her Göttingen lectures of 1929/30 but never published them. Deuring's lecture notes circulated. The first publication was a chapter of Hasse's Trans. AMS paper (1932), where the English term "crossed product" first appears. The theory was also included in Deuring's *Algebren* [R]. The resulting isomorphism Br(L/K) ≅ H²(Gal(L/K), L*) is how we state it today [R].

### (c) Representation theory as a feeder

This strand was missing from the first draft.

- **Noether** wanted representation theory absorbed into the theory of algebras. Her talk at the 1925 DMV meeting in Danzig treated Frobenius's group characters as the ideal theory of the group ring. Her later papers were *Hyperkomplexe Größen und Darstellungstheorie* (Math. Z. 30, 1929) and *Nichtkommutative Algebra* (Math. Z. 37, 1933) [R].
- **Brauer**, a student of Schur, came to algebras from the Schur index and splitting fields of representations. Noether persuaded him to work within the framework of algebras (her letter of 28 March 1927) [R].
- **The 1927 quaternion episode** [R]. In that letter Noether wrongly claimed that every minimal splitting field of a division algebra embeds in it. Brauer suspected that minimal splitting fields could have unbounded degree. Noether wanted to test this on the ordinary quaternions over ℚ, and on 5 October 1927 she asked Hasse. The next day Hasse sent a proof that such fields exist. It used his **local-global principle for quadratic forms** applied to x₁² + x₂² + x₃² + x₄²: a field splits the quaternions exactly when this form has a nontrivial zero there. The result was a joint note by Brauer and Noether with a companion note by Hasse (Sitzungsber. Berlin 1927). Roquette calls this the "nucleus" of the 1931 local-global principle for algebras.

### (d) "Arithmetic of algebras": copying Dedekind and Hensel (1920s)

- **Dickson**, *Algebras and their Arithmetics* (Chicago, 1923). It defines **maximal orders** and stresses them, but treats their arithmetic only in special Euclidean cases [R, AH].
- **Speiser**, "Allgemeine Zahlentheorie" (1926), started a general ideal theory. He arranged the German edition, *Algebren und ihre Zahlentheorie* (Zürich, 1927), which was reworked by Dickson and includes Speiser's chapter on ideal theory [R].
- **Artin**, three papers in the Hamburg *Abhandlungen* 5 (**1928**, not 1927 as the first draft said) [R, AH]:
  - a new proof of Wedderburn's theorem on finite division rings;
  - structure theory of rings with minimum condition (now "Artinian rings");
  - ideal theory of maximal orders, including Brandt's groupoid of ideals.
- **Hasse, "Über ℘-adische Schiefkörper und ihre Bedeutung für die Arithmetik hyperkomplexer Zahlsysteme"** (Math. Ann. 104, 1931, pp. 495–534; received 18 June 1930) [R, AH]. In Hensel's spirit Hasse localized algebras. He extended the p-adic valuation to a local division algebra D by v(x) = (1/n)·v(Nrd x) and proved that every central simple algebra of dimension n² over a p-adic field contains the unramified extension of degree n. So **local algebras are cyclic**, with an invariant in ℚ/ℤ, the **Hasse invariant** [R]. Hasse wrote that he was extending Speiser's idea "in the same sense as Hensel's arithmetic of number fields had done with the original idea of Kummer" [R]. Roquette calls the paper groundbreaking [AH].

### (e) The merger: the Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem (1931–32)

**Noether's program.** She had long argued for building norm residue theory purely locally through algebras [AH]. On a postcard of 12 April 1931 she asked Hasse for a "direct hypercomplex foundation of the invariants … and thus hypercomplex foundation of the reciprocity law!" [R].

**The pieces of the proof** [R]:

1. **The norm theorem.** For cyclic L/K, an element that is a norm everywhere locally is a global norm. For prime degree this is due to Hilbert (quadratic case) and Furtwängler (1902 ff.). Hasse proved it for all cyclic degrees in March 1931 (Göttinger Nachr. 1931), and showed it fails for non-cyclic abelian extensions, refuting his own earlier conjecture. Noether then told him that the right generalization concerns factor systems, i.e. algebras, not norms.
2. **Hasse's first attempts (summer 1931)** tried to reach the local-global principle for algebras through the trace form and his local-global principle for quadratic forms. He later dropped this. A Marburg conference on skew fields (26 Feb – 1 Mar 1931), organized by Hasse and Noether, had discussed his conjectures [AH].
3. **Reductions.** Brauer supplied a Sylow argument (letter of 29 Oct 1931) that reduces to algebras with a solvable splitting field. Noether supplied an induction (letter of 8 Nov 1931) that reduces to a cyclic splitting field of prime degree. So **only the Hilbert–Furtwängler norm theorem for prime degree is needed** [R].
4. **From local-global to cyclicity.** One also needs an **existence theorem**: a cyclic extension of given degree n with prescribed divisibility of local degrees at finitely many primes. Hasse sketched a proof to Albert in April 1931 but never published it. Grunwald proved a stronger version (Crelle 169, 1933). **Wang** found a counterexample to Grunwald's theorem in 1948, but the weaker statement BHN needs remains true (Hasse, Crelle 188, 1950). So the Main Theorem stands [R].

**The theorem.** R. Brauer, H. Hasse and E. Noether, **"Beweis eines Hauptsatzes in der Theorie der Algebren"**, J. reine angew. Math. 167 (1932), 399–404 [R, AH]. It appeared in the volume for Hensel's 70th birthday (29 Dec 1931). Roquette dates the decisive step to **9 November 1931**, and the manuscript reached the editors on 11 November [R]. Over a number field:

- **Local-global principle:** a central simple algebra that splits everywhere locally splits globally.
- **Main Theorem:** every central division algebra is cyclic ("of Dickson type").

Responses:

- **Artin:** "This is the greatest advance in number theory of the last years" (letter c. 11 Nov 1931) [R].
- **Siegel** (9 Dec 1931): "the most beautiful birthday present for Hensel, that his p-adic method has been granted such a triumph" [AH].

**Albert** reached the local-global principle independently. His paper was Bull. AMS 37 (1931), 777–784, submitted 9 Sept 1931. Albert asked for "some priority", so Hasse extended a footnote and proposed a joint paper [R]:

> A. A. Albert and H. Hasse, "A determination of all normal division algebras over an algebraic number field", Trans. AMS 34 (1932), 722–726.

Kaplansky wrote that Albert was "nosed out in a photo finish" [R].

### (f) Hasse 1933: reciprocity from noncommutative algebra

**H. Hasse, "Die Struktur der R. Brauerschen Algebrenklassengruppe über einem algebraischen Zahlkörper. Insbesondere Begründung der Theorie des Normenrestsymbols und Herleitung des Reziprozitätsgesetzes mit nichtkommutativen Hilfsmitteln"**, Math. Ann. 107 (1933), 731–760 [AH, R]. It is dedicated to Emmy Noether for her 50th birthday (23 March 1932), and the subtitle states the program [AH]. Its contents [R]:

- A systematic re-presentation of the BHN proof, answering Noether's complaint about the hastily written joint paper.
- The **Global Structure Theorem**: the sequence below is exact.

  0 → Br(K) → ⊕_v Br(K_v) → ℚ/ℤ → 0

  The second map is the sum of the local invariants.
- Part (i), **the sum of the invariants is 0**, is **equivalent to Artin's reciprocity law** [R]. Hasse had already found the ingredients in his Trans. AMS paper (1932) by comparing the invariant of (L/K, σ, a) with the norm symbol (a, L/K)_v. The additive sum formula corresponds to the multiplicative product formula.
- **The new step:** before this, the local norm symbol had been defined *through* Artin's global reciprocity law (Hasse 1927, 1930, 1931). In spring 1932 Hasse gave a **purely local definition** through the invariants of algebras. That let him prove the reciprocity law "almost entirely" from the theory of the Brauer group, fulfilling Noether's wish [R, AH].

At the 1932 Zürich ICM:

- Noether's plenary address was "Hyperkomplexe Systeme in ihren Beziehungen zur kommutativen Algebra und (zur) Zahlentheorie". Roquette's bibliography omits the second "zur"; MacTutor includes it. It appeared in *Verhandlungen* vol. 1, pp. 189–194 [R, W].
- Hasse spoke on "Strukturtheorie der halbeinfachen Algebren über algebraischen Zahlkörpern" [R].

## 3. What became of it: cohomology

- The BHN paper also showed that a Galois extension L/K is determined by Br(L/K). This raised the hope of a non-abelian class field theory through factor systems. Artin's attempts in March–May 1932 disappointed him: "I have the impression that something completely new has to be added" [R]. That route was eventually dropped [R].
- **Chevalley** introduced ideles and an algebraic approach to class field theory: J. Math. pures appl. (9) 15 (1936); Ann. of Math. 41 (1940) [AH].
- **Weil**, "Sur la théorie du corps de classes", J. Math. Soc. Japan 3 (1951), 1–35 [W].
- **Hochschild and Nakayama**, "Cohomology in class field theory", Ann. of Math. 55 (1952), 348–366. They showed how the Brauer-group arguments could be replaced by cohomological ones [W].
- The **Artin–Tate** seminar (1951–52; published as *Class Field Theory*, Benjamin, 1968) axiomatized class field theory cohomologically [R]. Roquette notes that Axiom I is essentially exactness of the sequence above at H²(K), and Axiom II is essentially exactness at H²(I_K) [R].

Hasse had brought simple algebras into class field theory; "these have survived in modern times as 2-cohomology classes only" [R]. The **fundamental class** u_{L/K} ∈ H²(Gal(L/K), L*) corresponds to an algebra class, and the reciprocity map is cup product with it (Tate–Nakayama) *(standard; not checked against a source here)*.

## 4. Corrections and additions after checking

- **Artin's three hypercomplex papers** date from **1928** (Abh. Hamburg 5), not 1927.
- **Dickson's cyclic algebras:** the first draft said "roughly 1906–1914". Albert dates the construction to 1905, and the paper is Trans. AMS 7 (1906). *Linear Algebras* (1914) is later and was not checked.
- **The BHN proof** needs the norm theorem **only for prime degree** (the Hilbert–Furtwängler theorem). It also needs an **existence theorem** for cyclic fields, which the first draft left out. That theorem connects to the Grunwald–Wang story.
- **"Brauer group"** is Hasse's name, from 1932.
- **Hasse's 1933 paper:** the first draft said "essentially Artin reciprocity". Roquette puts it more precisely: the sum formula is equivalent to Artin reciprocity, and the new step in 1933 was a *purely local* definition of the norm symbol through algebras.
- **Two strands added:** representation theory (Noether and Brauer), and the 1927 quaternion/splitting-field episode, which Roquette treats as the origin of the local-global principle for algebras.
- **Noether's ICM title** varies between sources ("und Zahlentheorie" vs. "und zur Zahlentheorie").
- **Deuring's *Algebren*:** Roquette dates it 1934. It is often cited as 1935, the Ergebnisse volume. Not resolved.
