## 1. Jánossy's *Theory of Relativity Based on Physical Reality* (1971)

### The book and its author

Lajos Jánossy (1912–1978) was a Hungarian physicist. He is best known for cosmic-ray research, done with Blackett in Manchester and later at the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies. He is also known for low-intensity photon interference experiments with Zs. Náray in Budapest. From the 1950s on he wrote a series of papers reinterpreting the Lorentz transformation. The book, published by Akadémiai Kiadó in Budapest, is the full statement of that program.

His motivation was mainly philosophical. He was a committed realist, and his realism was shaped by the dialectical-materialist climate in which he worked. He objected to what he saw as the positivist, measurement-based framing of Einstein's 1905 theory. On that framing, "moving rods are shorter" is a statement about the results of certain measurement procedures. Jánossy wanted every such result explained by physical processes in objects that exist whether or not anyone observes them.

### How his reading differs from Einstein's

Jánossy doesn't dispute the formulas or the predictions. He disputes what the formulas mean.

1. **A physical medium and a preferred frame.** Jánossy brings back an ether-like medium, essentially Lorentz's ether. In its rest frame, time and simultaneity are absolute. The other inertial frames are not physically equivalent to it. They only look equivalent from inside.

2. **The Lorentz transformation as a real physical change.** For Einstein, the Lorentz transformation relates two equally valid coordinate descriptions of the same events. Jánossy stresses a second reading. Take a physical system at rest, such as a rod, a clock or a whole laboratory, and set it moving. It turns into a different physical state, the "Lorentz-deformed" image of the original. Its rods really contract along the direction of motion, and its clocks really run slow, relative to the medium. He states this as a principle, sometimes called the Lorentz principle. It comes with conditions:
   - The acceleration must be gradual (quasi-static), or the body must be given time to relax afterwards.
   - The system must be governed by Lorentz-covariant laws.

   A body accelerated abruptly, or pushed unevenly at different points, is left strained and does not simply end up in the contracted state. So contraction is a dynamical result, not a guaranteed kinematic fact.

3. **The relativity principle is derived, not assumed.** Observers in a moving lab measure with rods and clocks that have themselves been deformed. They synchronize those clocks with light signals, following Einstein's convention. Their measurements therefore reproduce exactly the numbers the Lorentz transformation predicts, which is why they cannot detect their motion through the medium. For Einstein, the relativity principle and the constancy of light speed are postulates. For Jánossy, they follow from dynamics.

4. **Simultaneity.** For Jánossy, the relativity of simultaneity reflects the synchronization convention applied with deformed instruments. It says nothing deep about time itself. There is a fact about which events are simultaneous, namely simultaneity in the medium's frame, even if it can't be found by experiment. This links to the conventionality-of-simultaneity debate (Reichenbach, Grünbaum).

5. **Other consequences.**
   - *Twin paradox:* there's no symmetry to explain away. The travelling twin's clock was really slowed by its motion relative to the medium.
   - *Minkowski spacetime:* a useful mathematical tool, not a description of physical reality.

Within special relativity the two readings make the same predictions. The disagreement is about explanation, ontology and what guides further research.

## 2. Comparison with Bell

Bell's "How to Teach Special Relativity" (1976) is similar in spirit, and **Bell cites Jánossy's 1971 book in that article**. Bell had read the book, so the link is direct.

### Where and how Bell cites Jánossy

*Checked against the article as reprinted in* Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics, *pp. 67–80.*

The citation is note 8, near the end of the article. Just before it, Bell contrasts Einstein's approach with Lorentz's. The main text reads:

> "There is no intention here to make any reservation whatever about the power and precision of Einstein's approach. But in my opinion there is also something to be said for taking students along the road made by Fitzgerald, Larmor, Lorentz and Poincare⁸. The longer road sometimes gives more familiarity with the country."

Note 8 reads in full:

> "The only modern text-book taking essentially this road, among those with which I am acquainted, seems to be that of L. Janossy: Theory of Relativity Based on Physical Reality, Academiaia Kiado, Budapest (1971)."

So Bell cites Jánossy as a **pedagogical precedent**: the one modern textbook he knows that follows the FitzGerald–Larmor–Lorentz–Poincaré route. He doesn't discuss or endorse Jánossy's philosophy. The paragraph just before separates the two issues explicitly:

> "The facts of physics do not oblige us to accept one philosophy rather than the other. And we need not accept Lorentz's philosophy to accept a Lorentzian pedagogy. Its special merit is to drive home the lesson that the laws of physics in any one reference frame account for all physical phenomena, including the observations of moving observers."

Read in context, the note endorses Jánossy's *route* while declining to require his *ontology*. That supports the earlier guess that Bell's restraint was deliberate, though the note doesn't say why he held back.

### Where Bell and Jánossy agree

- **Dynamical explanation.** Bell models an atom with classical Maxwell–Lorentz electrodynamics, using Lorentz's momentum formula for the electron, and accelerates the nucleus gently. He shows its electron orbit contracts (FitzGerald) and its period lengthens (Larmor), all calculated in one frame. He then notes that Maxwell's and Lorentz's equations keep the same form under the Lorentz transformation. So for any state of a system at rest there is a corresponding "primed" state in motion, showing both effects.
- **How the acceleration happens matters.** Bell states this as his "second proviso," and it closely matches Jánossy's condition. Lorentz invariance only shows that a moving counterpart of the resting state *exists*. It doesn't show that a system *set in motion* will end up in that state rather than the counterpart of some other one. "A system set brutally in motion may be bruised, or broken, or heated, or burned." The contraction can only be assumed for "a coherent dynamical system whose configuration is determined essentially by internal forces and only little perturbed by gentle external forces."
- **The spaceship paradox.** Bell's version uses three spaceships. B and C accelerate identically on a signal from A, with a fragile thread tied between B and C. Seen from A, B and C stay a fixed distance apart, but the thread must FitzGerald-contract, so it eventually breaks. Bell recounts that a canvass of the CERN Theory Division produced "a clear consensus that the thread would not break!" He cites Dewan and Beran (1959), Evett and Wangsness (1960), Dewan (1963) and Evett (1972).
- **Moving observers are fooled by deformed instruments.** Bell's moving observer doesn't notice her metre sticks are contracted, "because the retina of her eye will also be contracted." She doesn't notice her clocks slowing, "because she will herself be thinking more slowly." Her light-signal synchronization "will mislead her," so that she "is led to think" the primed time is "the real time." This is very close to Jánossy's account (§1, point 3). Bell then makes it symmetric. She will say the resting observer's sticks are contracted and his clocks badly synchronized, and "Her view will be logically consistent and in perfect accord with the observable facts. He will have no way of persuading her that she is wrong."
- **Realism.** Bell's insistence on "beables" (in other writings) and his dislike of measurement-based formulations match Jánossy's "physical reality."

### Where they differ

- **Which frame.** For Bell, *any* inertial frame can serve as the frame where the dynamics is done. "Lorentzian pedagogy" needs no *particular* true frame. Jánossy makes the medium's frame physically special. Bell's version is therefore close to Harvey Brown's later dynamical approach, which keeps the dynamical explanation but drops any ether.
- **How strong the claim is.** Bell presented his approach as pedagogy. Einstein and Lorentz, he said, differ in "philosophy" and "style," not in predictions:
  - *Philosophy:* Lorentz believed in a state of real rest defined by the aether; Einstein declared "really resting" meaningless.
  - *Style:* Einstein starts from the single hypothesis that the laws look the same to all uniformly moving observers.

  In his "first proviso," Bell also admits that the Maxwell–Lorentz model is inadequate for real matter. It can't account for stability, radiation reaction or the strong interactions. So he assumes "with Lorentz that the complete theory is Lorentz invariant." Jánossy presented his reading as the correct interpretation. Given the political and philosophical baggage around Jánossy's program, which openly defended an ether and was framed as materialism, Bell's caution may have been partly strategic (speculation).
- **Why a preferred frame.** In interviews, notably in *The Ghost in the Atom* (1986), Bell did say he'd like to return to a Lorentz–Poincaré preferred frame. His reason was quantum nonlocality: something behind the scenes may travel faster than light, and a hidden preferred frame would make that coherent. So Bell ends up near Jánossy on the preferred-frame question, but from quantum foundations, not from a materialist critique of positivism. The 1976 article itself stays neutral on this.

## 3. Comparison with Rafelski

Johann Rafelski has described Bell's influence, going back to his CERN years. It shows most clearly in *Relativity Matters* (2017).

**What Rafelski takes from Bell:**

- He treats Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction as a real, material "body contraction," a dynamical effect in a body brought into motion.
- He discusses the spaceship paradox in Bell's style.

**Where he parts from Jánossy: the medium.**

- Rafelski also brings back an "ether," as does his earlier book with Berndt Müller, *The Structured Vacuum* (1985).
- For him the ether is the **quantum vacuum** of field theory, with its condensates, fluctuations and response to strong fields.
- That vacuum is **Lorentz-invariant**. It is a physical medium, but it picks out no rest frame and gives no absolute simultaneity.
- This follows Einstein's 1920 Leiden lecture (space without ether is unthinkable, but that ether has no state of motion) and Dirac's 1951 *Nature* letter.

Rafelski has also suggested that the vacuum structure may act as a reference for *acceleration*: which motions count as inertial and which as accelerated. It would not be a reference for velocity. This point is less certain; check against his texts.

Since Rafelski got his "body contraction" view from Bell, and Bell cites Jánossy, Jánossy's emphasis on material deformation reached Rafelski indirectly. It is not known here whether Rafelski engages with Jánossy directly.

## 4. The views side by side

| | Einstein (1905) | Bell | Rafelski | Jánossy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contraction and dilation | Kinematic, measurement-related | Dynamical, calculable in one frame | Dynamical "body contraction" | Real physical deformation relative to the medium |
| Relativity principle | Postulate | Consequence of Lorentz-covariant dynamics | Fundamental symmetry, also realized dynamically | Consequence of dynamics plus measuring conventions |
| Physical medium | None (1905) | Not needed, but favored for quantum reasons | Yes: Lorentz-invariant quantum vacuum | Yes: medium with a rest frame |
| Preferred frame / absolute simultaneity | No | Not needed for teaching; privately sympathetic | No (possibly for acceleration only) | Yes, though undetectable |
| Framing of the claim | Physical theory | Teaching approach, "difference of style" | Physical and teaching approach | Correct ontology |

In short, all three share the view that relativistic effects need a physical, dynamical explanation. They split on the medium. Jánossy's medium breaks Lorentz symmetry at the level of what physically exists, while keeping it at the level of what can be observed. Rafelski's medium fully respects the symmetry. Bell's teaching argument needs no medium at all, but for quantum reasons he leaned toward Jánossy's side.
