dirichlet-rank-one.gp -- the unit theorem where the rank is one

  (1) the two families of unit rank one
      field                     disc      sig     rank  mu(K)  Galois?
      Q(sqrt 2   )             8         [2, 0]  1     2      yes
      Q(sqrt 3   )             12        [2, 0]  1     2      yes
      Q(sqrt 5   )             5         [2, 0]  1     2      yes
      Q(sqrt 61  )             61        [2, 0]  1     2      yes
      Q[x]/(x^3 - x - 1       ) -23        [1, 1]  1     2      no
      Q[x]/(x^3 - 2           ) -108       [1, 1]  1     2      no
      Q[x]/(x^3 - x^2 + x + 1 ) -44        [1, 1]  1     2      no
      A Galois cubic is CYCLIC, and complex conjugation would be an
      automorphism of order 2 in a group of order 3, hence trivial: so a
      Galois cubic is TOTALLY REAL and has unit rank 2.  Every rank-one
      cubic is therefore non-Galois -- the conjugates do not lie in K.

  (2) discreteness with no lattice theory: units in a bounded range
      real quadratic: u > 1 a unit ==> u + u' = Tr(u) in Z and u u' = +-1,
      so u is a root of X^2 - m X + s, s = +-1, |m| <= T + 1.  Enumerating
      those polynomials directly must reproduce the powers of eps:
      d       eps                #units in (1,T] by bounded m   = floor(log T / log eps)?
      2       2.4142135624       7                             7
      3       3.7320508076       5                             5
      5       1.6180339887       14                            14
      13      3.3027756377       5                             5
      61      39.0256241898      1                             1
      complex cubic: |N(u)| = sigma_1(u).|sigma_2(u)|^2 = 1 pins
      |sigma_2(u)| = sigma_1(u)^(-1/2), so ONE real parameter controls all
      three conjugates -- which is what unit rank one means.  The three
      elementary symmetric functions are then bounded, so again finitely many.
      f                    eps at the real place  |sigma_2(eps)|   product
      x^3 - x - 1          1.3247179572           0.8688369618     1.0000000000
      x^3 - 2              3.8473221019           0.5098245285     1.0000000000
      x^3 - x^2 + x + 1    1.8392867552           0.7373527058     1.0000000000

  (3) existence for real quadratic: Dirichlet approximation + pigeonhole
      d      max |p^2-dq^2|  bound 1+2 sqrt d   collision   unit found   = eps^j
      2      1               3.828427           (1,3)       5.828427     eps^2
      3      2               4.464102           (1,3)       3.732051     eps^1
      5      1               5.472136           (1,3)       17.944272    eps^6
      7      3               6.291503           (1,9)       253.996063   eps^2
      13     4               8.211103           (1,11)      1297.999230  eps^6
      61     12              16.620499          (1,23)      3532638098.000000 eps^6
      94     15              20.390719          (8,24)      4286590.000000 eps^1
      Every |p^2 - d q^2| stays under 1 + 2 sqrt d, so the box principle
      alone -- no Minkowski, no logarithmic embedding -- produces a unit
      of infinite order.  With (2) that IS the unit theorem here.

  (4) existence for a complex cubic: the same proof, one dimension up
      f                    Q range   max |N(alpha)|  collision   unit found      |N(u)|  = eps^j
      x^3 - x - 1          2..40     1               (1,2)        0.32471796      1       eps^-4
      x^3 - 2              2..40     6               (5,6)        0.25992105      1       eps^-1
      x^3 - x^2 + x + 1    2..40     2               (1,3)        0.16071324      1       eps^-3
      x^3 - 3              2..40     16              (3,33)       0.08008382      1       eps^-1
      |N(alpha)| stays bounded as Q grows -- that is the whole content --
      and the collision produces a unit of infinite order.  Nothing here is
      harder than (3) except that the pigeonhole is 2-dimensional.

  (5) the sign of N(eps): an invariant in one case, not in the other
      N(-1) = (-1)^n : quadratic 1, cubic -1
      d with N(eps) = -1 (negative Pell soluble) vs N(eps) = +1:
        N = -1 : [2, 5, 10, 13, 17, 26, 29]
        N = +1 : [3, 6, 7, 11, 15, 19, 46]
      Necessary: every prime factor of d is 2 or = 1 (mod 4) -- else -1 is a
      non-residue mod some p | d.  NOT sufficient, and no congruence in d can
      decide it; the smallest counterexamples pass the test and still fail:
        all factors 2 or 1 mod 4, yet N(eps) = +1 : [34, 146, 194, 205, 221]
      complex cubic: eps and -eps have opposite norms, so +1 is always
      available.  Both signs realised on the same field:
      f                    N(eps)   N(-eps)
      x^3 - x - 1          -1        1
      x^3 - 2              -1        1
      x^3 - 3              1        -1

  (6) Artin's inequality for complex cubics:  |d_K| <= 4 eps^3 + 24
      403 complex cubic fields with |d_K| <= 3000: 0 violations
      sup |d_K|/(4 eps^3 + 24) = 0.993632, attained at x^3 + 7*x - 1 -- essentially sharp
      So eps >= ((|d_K| - 24)/4)^(1/3): the fundamental unit of a complex
      cubic is bounded BELOW by the discriminant.  No such bound holds for
      real quadratics, where eps is bounded below only by ~ sqrt d.

  (7) how big the regulator gets, in the two families
      real quadratic, d <= 1000   : max R =  68.8018 at d = 991      (R/sqrt d = 2.1856)
      complex cubic, |d_K| <= 1000  : max R =  12.5106 at d = -999      (R/log|d| = 1.8114)
      In this range the real quadratic regulator runs away at the scale of
      sqrt d, the complex cubic one at the scale of log |d|.  The lower bound
      is a theorem (Artin, check 6); the upper contrast is only what is
      observed here -- h.R grows like |d|^(1/2) in both families, and it is
      how the product splits between h and R that differs.

  (8) no rank-one cubic is Galois -- the structural difference
      403 complex cubic fields with |d_K| <= 3000; Galois ones: 0
      A cubic Galois over Q is cyclic; complex conjugation would then be an
      element of order 2 in a group of order 3, hence trivial -- so a Galois
      cubic is totally real, with unit rank 2.  Consequence for the proof:
      in Q(sqrt d) the conjugate u' lies IN the field, so Tr(u) and N(u) are
      visible inside K and the whole argument of (2)-(3) happens there.  In a
      complex cubic the other two conjugates live outside K and one must work
      in the Minkowski embedding.  That, and the 2-dimensional pigeonhole, is
      the entire extra cost.
